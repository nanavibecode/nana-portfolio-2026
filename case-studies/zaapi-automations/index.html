Executive Summary
The Situation
As of June 2024, Zaapi had no automation at all — in a Thai SaaS market where competitors already offered it. That hurt user efficiency and response speed, left Zaapi behind on feature parity, and put retention at risk.
The Problem
Customer service managers had to hit a different SLA on every marketplace — Shopee: reply within 12 hours, 24/7; Lazada: within 10 minutes, 8 AM–11 PM — with agents who didn't want to work after hours and no appetite to hire more. The market's answer was complex flowchart builders that needed logic and technical know-how to set up — locking out exactly the managers who needed automation most.

Context

Automation was table stakes Zaapi didn't have yet

Automations had three jobs: close the gap versus competitors already offering it in Thai SaaS, add a feature sticky enough to boost agent efficiency and drive renewals, and lay the foundation for what came next — segmented broadcasts, comment capture, and eventually AI. The vision map, sketched before any of it existed, put automations as the first line of defense: handling common enquiries so agents didn't have to.

The competition wasn't one product. Sellers were stitching tools together — one manager we interviewed had tried ZWIZ.AI, BigSeller, ManyChat, Pancake, Zapier, and Lookalike, and was running Zaapi for their marketplace team and Pancake for their social team at the same time. Meanwhile the marketplaces' own native tools had gaps: card messages were hard to create, and there was no way to build custom flows. Zaapi didn't need to out-feature any single tool; it needed to be the one place a whole team would actually set automation up.

Problem

Three people, one shared pressure: keep up without more headcount

Five exploratory interviews with sellers, agents, and managers — synthesized into an empathy map and journey map per persona — surfaced three people under the same pressure from different sides.

Khun Nookie — Simplified Process Manager

Pain points
  • Must meet every marketplace's SLA, each with different rules
  • No agents want to work outside business hours — and hiring more costs money and training time
  • "Setting up the flow builder is difficult. I can do it, but it will take time."

Khun Tung — Tech-Savvy Manager

Pain points
  • Comfortable building flows from scratch — wants to automate as much as possible
  • Juggling several tools to split social and marketplace teams
  • Bot replies feel patterned and robotic, and struggle with the complexity of Thai

Nong Fern — Missing-Chat Concerned Agent

Pain points
  • Can't keep up with chat volume on campaign days
  • Doesn't want to answer customers after hours — needs real time off
  • Worried automations might mess up marketplace SLA calculations

The core finding cut across all three: most users wanted simple automations that covered their essential needs, and were overwhelmed by too much customization. That turned out to be the key to adoption.

My Role

What I owned, end to end

As Zaapi's only product designer, I led the UX initiative end to end — framing the research questions, running the customer interviews, building empathy maps and journey maps, prioritizing features against users' technical comfort, sketching and exploring low- and high-fidelity designs, usability testing, and writing the handover engineering built from across both phases. I partnered with the Head of Product and a five-engineer tech team, on a one-week design cadence alongside engineering's two-week sprints. Design Process

Double Diamond — research before solutions

I use the Double Diamond as my framework: research on-site with customers and users first, before designing anything. For Automations that meant sitting with sellers, agents, and managers before a single screen got sketched.

What the research had to answer

  • Workflows and pain points of each persona in chat-based customer service, split by Manager and Chat Agent roles — to design the features.
  • Which automations organizations actually needed — to prioritize them.
  • How familiar less tech-savvy users really were with automation — an assumption we needed to test, not design around.

Discover and Define covered the five interviews and the empathy and journey maps behind the three personas. Develop and Deliver covered prioritization, sketching, hi-fi exploration, and usability testing, through to what shipped in Phase 1 and Phase 2.

Key Decisions

What I chose, and why

Three decisions shaped whether this feature would get adopted at all, let alone earn a Phase 2 — one that deliberately gave up parity with competitors, one about where automation lives, and one about waiting.

Templates, not a flowchart — even though competitors had one

Alternative: matching the market with a full flowchart / logic builder and comprehensive customization from day one

Competitor tools answered automation with complex flowcharts that needed logic and technical understanding to set up. Khun Tung could handle that — but Khun Nookie said it outright: setting up a flow builder was possible, but it would take time — and time was exactly what that manager didn't have. So Phase 1 launched with four pre-defined templates with an instant preview — auto-assignment, automated greeting and out-of-hours messages, comment reply, and label tagging — that anyone could switch on. Fewer variables also meant faster speed to market. The competitive cost was real: for a while, power users had deeper tools elsewhere. Rapid usability testing justified it — the templates met over 80% of user needs with zero learning curve, and users described setup as "like filling out a form." [Did anyone — sales, larger prospects, the Head of Product — push to build the full flow builder first to match competitors? How was it settled?]

Give automation its own home, organized by goal

Alternative: placing it inside team management for an effortless entry point, and presenting templates in one all-in-one modal vs. dedicated navigation tabs per automation type

Both placements and both presentations were sketched and prototyped. What shipped: Automations as its own item in the main navigation, and a "create new automation" modal that groups templates by the job a manager is trying to do — Engage with customers, Timely response guarantee (SLA for Shopee, Lazada, and TikTok), and Team collaboration — rather than by technical trigger, with "Suggested for you" templates on the list page. [Confirm the reasoning: why did its own nav item beat team management, and why goal-based categories in a modal over per-type tabs?]

Earn Phase 2 with evidence, not requests

Alternative: building the flow builder right after Phase 1, since usability testing already showed demand for advanced logic and more automation types

Testing surfaced the demand immediately — requests for label, comment, and chatbot automations, and pain around advanced logic. But requests from a few power users aren't a roadmap. We spent roughly six months analyzing results before committing, against three signals: add-on subscriptions (real demand), customization requests (real need for custom workflows), and company direction (the strategy to win larger customers). Feedback across different company types showed it was larger companies that needed advanced workflow customization — which is what turned the Flow Builder from a wish list into Phase 2.

Solution & Delivery

What shipped

A two-phase no-code automation system: four simple templates anyone could turn on with zero learning curve, and a full drag-and-drop flow builder for teams that outgrew them.

Phase 1 — Basic automation templates

Four core templates — auto-assignment, automated greeting and out-of-hours messages, comment reply, and label tagging — set up through a one-page form with a live chat preview, so there was nothing to learn.

What usability testing told us

  • Basic automations are key — confirmed as users' most immediate need, matching their current workflows.
  • Straightforward usability — "like filling out a form," with the chat preview on the right showing exactly what customers would see.
  • Platform gaps to fill — native marketplace tools made card messages hard and offered no custom flows, an opening for Zaapi.
  • Appetite for more — requests for more pre-built templates: label, comment, and chatbot automations.

Phase 2 — Custom Flow Builder

Once Phase 1 proved demand, Phase 2 introduced a drag-and-drop visual flow system for large teams building complex logic — modular trigger → filter → action cards, so power users could build custom automations without engineering. I designed the entry points, the flow-creation UX, and the system feedback, plus the design-system pieces it runs on: automation cards, trigger-action blocks, custom chat previews, and reusable layouts for rule setting, condition blocks, and fallback flows.

Handover

Documented for engineering around four questions — why we're building it, who it's for, what the flows are, and how we're building it (scope and prioritization) — with annotated specs for component behaviors and states across both phases, so what shipped in Phase 2 could build cleanly on what Phase 1 had already proven.

The bigger picture

Automations was built to be sticky, and it landed in the same half-year Zaapi's renewal rate nearly doubled — from 33.17% (January–June 2024) to 63.68% (July–November 2024). I don't credit that to automations alone; it shipped alongside the AI and analytics work aimed at the same goal. But it's the outcome the feature existed for, and the Flow Builder that followed was aimed squarely at the larger companies Zaapi was starting to win.

What I'd improve

Prioritizing delivery over discovery paid off — demand was already proven, so shipping a minimum lovable product around the highest-volume use cases is what got Phase 1 to 80% coverage with zero learning curve, and serving the majority first bought the evidence for Phase 2. What I'd change is how the two phases connect. Basic Automations and the Flow Builder ended up as two separate systems, so moving up means rebuilding. Next time I'd design the migration path in Phase 1: upgrading should be a single click, preserving triggers, conditions, actions, and history — with the Flow Builder as the one interface for creating and editing automations, and Basic Automations simply seeding a pre-filled flow inside it. The same pattern showed up in the AI agent's deployment, which also started as a simple toggle and later moved into the Flow Builder. When I sequence simple-then-advanced now, I design the advanced system first and let the simple version be a view of it.
