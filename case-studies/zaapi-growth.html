Context

A Thai SaaS built for one market, asked to win a bigger one

Zaapi is a conversational commerce and customer-service platform — unifying chat, AI agents, automations, and analytics for teams selling across Shopee, Lazada, LINE, and other channels. It grew fast domestically, but the product carried assumptions specific to the Thai market: workflows, terminology, and dashboards built around how one type of team worked, not the range of roles and markets Zaapi needed to serve next.

My job was to lead product design across the platform as Zaapi expanded internationally — starting with understanding who was actually using it, and why the product needed to flex.

Research

One product, three very different jobs to be done

Interviews and workflow mapping across existing customers surfaced three distinct user roles sharing the same login — each with different responsibilities, KPIs, and reasons to open the dashboard. Designing for "the user" wasn't going to work; the product needed to serve all three without feeling built for none of them.

Design Process

Designing for the role, not just the screen

With three roles and their KPIs mapped, the design process centered on what each role needed surfaced first — same underlying data, different entry points and emphasis. Flows were stress-tested against real support scenarios: a spike in chats, a flagged case, a campaign under-performing — not just the happy path.

Wireframes & Validation

Validating with the teams who'd actually use it

Low-fidelity flows were walked through with customer service managers and business owners before any visual design — catching mismatches in mental models early, while they were still cheap to fix.

Final Interfaces

What shipped

A redesigned chat workspace, a rebuilt AI agent experience, a visual automation flow builder, and analytics that speak to each role's own KPIs — shipped incrementally as the platform scaled internationally.

Handover

Built to actually ship

Componentized in Figma with documented states and edge cases, so engineering could build without guessing — a requirement once the roadmap meant shipping across multiple markets in parallel.
